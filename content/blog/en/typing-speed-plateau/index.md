Many people have typed every day for years and still don't get any faster. That isn't a natural limit. As soon as typing works "well enough", it runs on autopilot, and on autopilot you don't learn anything new. To get faster again, you need to spend a while practising deliberately just above your usual speed, work on your weakest letter sequences and plan breaks to let it sink in.

## The "OK plateau"

The science writer Joshua Foer coined a term for this: the OK plateau. When you learn something new, you make quick progress at first and put in conscious effort. Once the big mistakes disappear, the activity becomes automatic. From then on you stop thinking about it, and that is exactly when improvement stops.

Typing shows this very clearly. You type emails and messages every day, but always at the same comfortable speed. That is repetition, not practice. The psychologist Anders Ericsson, whose research Foer draws on, puts it this way: such limits are usually not innate, we set them ourselves through the way we practise.

As early as 1897, the psychologists Bryan and Harter described long phases without measurable progress in telegraphers learning Morse code. Plateaus are a well-known pattern in learning skills, not a personal failure.

## How to recognise a plateau

- Your speed has been in the same range for weeks, no matter how much you type.
- Certain words or letter sequences slow you down again and again.
- When you try to type faster, mistakes shoot up right away.
- Practice feels effortless. That sounds good, but it often means it no longer challenges you.

## 1. Type just above your speed

For typing, Ericsson recommends deliberately pushing yourself about 10 to 20 percent above your usual speed, with full concentration. That reveals the letter sequences that fall apart at higher speed. Those are exactly what holds you back.

The dose matters. At 10 percent faster, mistakes rise a little; at 40 percent faster you are just practising chaos. And fast practice doesn't replace clean practice: alternate between short, fast runs and calm, accurate ones.

## 2. Find the sequences that slow you down

A plateau rarely involves all keys. Usually it is a few letter pairs where you hesitate or miss, such as "ght", "ck" or sequences with the pinky. Fast typists overlap their keystrokes: the next key is already down before the previous one is released. According to the [study of 136 million keystrokes](https://userinterfaces.aalto.fi/136Mkeystrokes/), that only works with well-practised letter sequences. Every shaky sequence breaks the flow.

Practise these sequences on purpose in short word lists, slowly and cleanly first, then faster. How to find your typical mistakes is explained in [Why you keep making the same typos](/blog/why-you-keep-making-the-same-typos/).

## 3. Short and focused instead of long and distracted

You can't keep up full concentration for long while typing. Ericsson gives about 15 to 30 minutes a day for untrained typists. More brings little once attention fades.

A classic study by Baddeley and Longman from 1978 also shows that short sessions work better. Postal workers learned to type on a new keyboard. Those who practised one hour a day learned faster per hour of practice and typed better in the end than the group with two sessions of two hours a day. The total time was the same.

## 4. Give your brain time to consolidate

Motor skills also consolidate between practice sessions, especially during sleep. In a [study by Matthew Walker](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2002/10/matthew-walker), participants became about 20 percent faster at a finger sequence after a night's sleep, without further practice. Those who waited 12 hours awake gained 2 percent.

For you this means: rather 15 minutes every day than two hours once a week. And if a difficult sequence won't work today, stop and try again tomorrow.

## 5. Practise with real text

Exercises with single letter pairs build up the movement; real sentences transfer it to everyday life. There you also get capitals, punctuation and unfamiliar words. Mix both: targeted exercises for the weak spots, with whole sentences in between.

## 6. Check your basics

Sometimes the plateau comes from a habit that doesn't matter at low speed:

- **One finger does another's work**, for example the ring finger for pinky keys. Beyond a certain speed it can't keep up.
- **Your eyes still wander down**, for example for digits or symbols. More on this in [Learn to type without looking at the keyboard](/blog/learn-to-type-without-looking/).
- **Your hands leave the home row** and have to find their way back afterwards.

Relearning such habits costs speed at first. After that, though, your limit is higher.
