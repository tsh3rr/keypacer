Typos are not random. Almost everyone makes the same ones again and again: "teh" instead of "the", an "r" instead of an "e", a missing letter in a double "ll". Research has known these patterns for decades. They come about because your fingers have learned certain movements wrongly or mix them up under time pressure. The good news: anything that follows a pattern can be practised on purpose.

## The four kinds of typos

Research distinguishes four basic types:

| Kind of error | Example | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substitution | "hpuse" instead of "house" | A finger hits the wrong key. |
| Insertion | "housse" | One keystroke too many, often doubled or a neighbouring key along with it. |
| Omission | "hose" | A keystroke is missing, often with double letters. |
| Transposition | "huose" | Two letters in the wrong order. |

Which errors you make depends on your skill. In 1983 the psychologist Jonathan Grudin [compared beginners with skilled typists](https://gwern.net/doc/design/typography/1983-grudin.pdf). For beginners, three quarters of all errors were substitutions: the fingers don't yet know for sure where the keys are. The skilled typists made many more insertions, and the picture varied a lot from person to person. So everyone has their own typical mistakes.

## Substitutions: usually the neighbouring key

When a finger hits the wrong key, it is usually right next to the correct one. In Grudin's data this was about 60 percent of substitutions for beginners and about a third for skilled typists. Typical pairs are <kbd>e</kbd> and <kbd>r</kbd>, <kbd>n</kbd> and <kbd>m</kbd> or <kbd>i</kbd> and <kbd>o</kbd>.

A second, rarer source is the mirror key on the other hand. Beginners sometimes use the middle finger of the wrong hand: a <kbd>k</kbd> instead of a <kbd>d</kbd>. The brain picked the right finger, but on the wrong side.

Neighbouring-key errors show that a finger doesn't yet have a fixed path to the key. It estimates the position instead of knowing it.

## Transpositions: when your hands overtake each other

"teh" instead of "the", "adn" instead of "and": transpositions look like careless mistakes, but they follow a clear rule. For skilled typists, about 80 percent of them happen between the hands, according to Grudin. So the two letters are on different sides of the keyboard.

The reason: when you type fluently, both hands prepare their keystrokes in parallel. If one hand is a fraction too early, its letter comes first. The faster you type, the more often this happens. Transpositions are a sign that your speed is above what the movement can carry cleanly.

## Why mistakes stick

Your fingers learn every movement you repeat, wrong ones included. If you type "teh" a hundred times and correct it, you have practised the wrong order a hundred times. Three things make it worse:

- **Too much speed.** Under time pressure your fingers fall back on whatever is quickest to hand, not on what is right.
- **Fatigue.** As concentration fades, the error rate rises, and you mostly practise mistakes.
- **No look at the patterns.** If you just delete every mistake, you don't notice that it is often the same three or four.

## Targeted practice instead of plain repetition

The psychologist Anders Ericsson studied how people get better at a skill. His best-known result from 1993: it is not the sheer amount of practice that counts, but targeted practice, known as "deliberate practice". That means working on exactly the weak spots, with a clear goal and immediate feedback. For typing this means:

1. **Find your patterns.** For a week, note which mistakes you make, or let a trainer count them. Usually a few keys and letter pairs cause most of the errors.
2. **Practise the pairs, not just the keys.** A mistake with "th" is a problem of sequence, not of a single letter. Practise words with the pair in them: the, that, with, other, think.
3. **Slow down until it sits.** Type the weak pair slowly enough that it works without mistakes, and only then raise the speed.
4. **Count the first attempt.** A mistake you fix right away is still a mistake. Only then do you see what really sits.
5. **Repeat with gaps.** A pair that works today can wobble again in three days. Short repetitions over several days make it stick.

Watch out for hesitation, too. A key where you pause briefly is often the next mistake. Slow keystrokes reveal weak spots before they turn into errors.
