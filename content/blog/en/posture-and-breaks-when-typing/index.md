If you type a lot, you often sit in the same position for hours. Most people only notice when their wrists, shoulders or neck start to ache. A few adjustments to your desk and regular breaks go a long way towards preventing that.

This article is not medical advice. If pain or tingling in your hands does not go away after a few days, get it checked.

## Keep your wrists straight

The most important rule: your hand, wrist and forearm should form a roughly straight line while you type. Two positions put the most strain on your wrists:

- **Bent upwards.** This happens when the heels of your hands rest on the desk or a wrist rest and your fingers reach up to the keys.
- **Bent sideways.** This happens when the keyboard is very narrow and your hands angle outwards towards its edges.

The fold-out feet at the back of many keyboards make the upward bend worse. A keyboard that lies flat is usually the better choice.

## Where your keyboard should sit

- **At elbow height or slightly below.** Your upper arms hang loosely and your elbows are bent at about a right angle.
- **Close to your body.** Reaching forward tenses your shoulders.
- **Centred in front of you.** On a keyboard with a number pad, centre the letter keys on your body, not the whole keyboard. A keyboard without a number pad lets your mouse sit closer.
- **Screen at eye level.** The top line of text should be at about eye level or a little below. A screen that is too low pulls your head forward.

This is hard on a laptop, because the keyboard and screen are attached. If you work on one for long stretches, raise it on a stand and plug in an external keyboard.

## Type lightly

Many people hit the keys harder than they need to. Light, even pressure is enough to register a keystroke. Typing more lightly takes strain off your fingers and tendons, and it usually makes your typing calmer too. Slow, clean practice is the best way to learn it.

Wrist rests are meant for the pauses between typing. While you type, your hands are better off hovering just above them.

## Why breaks help

Muscles and tendons recover mainly when they change position. Short, frequent breaks help more than one long break at the end of the day:

- **Stand up briefly every half hour.** Walk a few steps, roll your shoulders, shake out your hands.
- **Take your hands off the desk now and then.** For example while you read or think.
- **Look into the distance.** It gives your eyes a rest after hours of focusing at the same distance.

Breaks help with learning too. If you keep practising while tired, you make more mistakes and train them in. Short sessions with breaks get you further than long ones.

## How touch typing helps

If you type with two fingers, you keep looking down at the keyboard and tilt your head forward to do it. With touch typing your eyes stay on the screen and your neck stays straighter. Your keystrokes are also spread across ten fingers instead of two, and your hands stay on the home row instead of jumping around the keyboard.
